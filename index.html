AI-Aware Assignment Design Studio

From Detecting AI to Designing Visible Learning

A working toolkit for MSc(TDLL) instructors and programme teams. It helps you decide, before an assignment is published, where generative AI is permitted and what evidence of learning you will actually receive — then helps students describe their use honestly, and helps colleagues judge ambiguous cases consistently. Nothing here detects AI. It is a design instrument.

2 What must be visible in the student's work?

If an outcome cannot be seen in the submitted work, the design — not the student — needs attention.

3 If generative AI did this part for the student, would the learning still be assessed?

Design prompt. Restrict this use, ask for supporting evidence, revise the task, or assess the capability another way. Whichever you choose, say so in the statement students read.

Where is AI allowed?

One decision per stage, four options only. Enough to be clear; not enough to become a policy document.

4 Permission by stage *

One choice for every stage — nothing is left blank. Where a stage does not occur in this assignment, choose Not applicable; that is a decision, not a gap.

5 What will you accept as evidence? *

Select the minimum you need to make a fair judgement. Every item you tick is work a student must do, so tick deliberately.

6 How much extra time will that ask of a student?

Check the evidence is proportionate to the weighting. Heavier process requirements than the assignment is worth are a common reason students disengage from disclosure.

How will disclosure count?

Say what students must tell you, and what you will do with it. Vagueness here is what students read as risk.

Student AI Disclosure and Critical Reflection

This form helps explain how generative AI contributed to your work, how you checked its outputs, and what intellectual decisions remain your own. Complete only the sections required by your assignment.

Disclosure is responsible academic practice, not a confession. Declaring permitted use will not by itself reduce your grade. This form asks what you did, how you checked it, and what you decided.
Mode

Assignment and student

Only record what the assignment requires. Do not enter personal, confidential or sensitive information about yourself or anyone else.

Tools and extent of use

Did you use a generative AI tool in preparing this submission? *
Thank you. No AI-use details are required. Please continue to the declaration at the end of this form. If any tool was used after all — including a writing assistant you did not think of as AI — you can change this answer at any time.
That is a reasonable answer, not a problem. Generative AI tools include chat assistants, tools built into word processors or search engines that draft text, image generators, code assistants and transcription tools. Recording the tool name below is enough — no judgement is made here.

Tools used

Add one entry per tool. If you used the same tool at two different stages, add it twice.

Approximate extent of use (optional)
The extent of AI use does not by itself establish the quality or acceptability of the work. You are not asked for a percentage.

Verification

Three questions about how you checked what the tool produced. Answer about what you actually did, not what you intended to do.

Judgement and revision

The decisions you made about what the tool offered you.

What remains yours in this submission.

Review and export

For instructors — separate from student completion.
  • Interpret disclosure against the published assignment requirements.
  • Honest disclosure should not automatically reduce a grade.
  • Disclosure does not automatically demonstrate achievement.
  • Grade AI-related performance only where it is connected to an intended learning outcome and published rubric criterion.
  • Treat possible misconduct through the applicable institutional process rather than deciding it from this form alone.

Limitations

This toolkit can support clearer design and more informed judgement. It cannot conclusively prove authorship, replace applicable institutional procedures, or remove the need for contextual professional judgement.

What it can do

  • Make AI expectations specific enough to be followed.
  • Make the evidence requirement proportional and predictable.
  • Give students a way to describe judgement, not just quantity.
  • Give markers a shared vocabulary for ambiguous cases.
  • Surface assignment-design weaknesses before students are affected.

What it cannot do

  • Establish who wrote any particular sentence.
  • Replace AI-detection tools in every situation, or justify abandoning them entirely.
  • Show that a well-completed form means learning occurred.
  • Substitute for formal academic-integrity procedures.
  • Guarantee consistency between courses unless colleagues share the same clause language.

Conceptual foundations

Two frameworks inform the structure of the toolkit. Neither is quoted at length in the interface; both shaped what it asks for.

Caspari-Sadeghi's framework for teacher educators identifies three areas of capacity. They map onto the toolkit as follows.

  • Foundational understanding of GenAI capabilities and limitations. The checklist asks you to name the learning that must come from the student, then decide which stages AI may touch. Neither question can be answered without knowing what a tool does and where it fails. The scenario cards on fabricated references, AI translation and coding support test the same knowledge from the other direction.
  • Practical application to teaching and assessment design. The permission grid, the evidence choice and the marking decision are the operational core of the checklist.
  • Evaluation, ethics, fairness and human agency. The four concepts kept deliberately separate, the three fairness checks, and the debrief structure of every scenario card.

Source. Caspari-Sadeghi, S. AI literacy for teacher educators: a holistic curriculum for capacity-building in higher education.

Domínguez Figaredo and Stoyanovich argue that AI literacy should be designed around the different responsibilities of the people involved, rather than around technical knowledge alone. The toolkit reflects three stakeholder positions.

  • Instructor. Sets permissions, chooses evidence, decides what disclosure is worth — and judges against published criteria (the checklist and the scenario cards).
  • Student. Discloses use, verifies outputs, decides what to keep (the disclosure form).
  • Programme or institution. Needs consistency across courses and a separate route for possible breaches (the scenario cards on ambiguity and prohibited use; the instructor note at the end of the disclosure form).

Source. Domínguez Figaredo, D., and Stoyanovich, J. Responsible AI literacy: A stakeholder-first approach.

Help and facilitator guidance

Using this toolkit

Everything here runs in your browser. Nothing is sent anywhere, and nothing requires an account.

The three tools can be used independently, but they work best in sequence. If you are preparing one assignment, start with the checklist. If you are running a workshop, start with a scenario card and use the checklist afterwards to fix what the discussion exposed.

Two of the tools are for staff and one is for students. The checklist and the scenario cards are yours; the disclosure form is what a student completes at submission. Work saves automatically as you type.

  1. Before the semester. Complete the checklist for each assignment (10–15 minutes each).
  2. At release. Publish the generated statement with the brief. Keep a copy in your course site.
  3. At submission. Ask students to complete the disclosure in the mode the assignment requires.
  4. At marking. Read the disclosure alongside the rubric — but only against published criteria.
  5. In the programme team. Use one scenario card per meeting to calibrate judgement across courses.
  6. After the term. Record one design change per assignment. That is the compounding benefit.

Generative AI
A system that produces new text, images, code or audio in response to a prompt, rather than only retrieving existing material.
Task-level permission
A decision about one specific stage of an assignment — outlining, drafting, editing — rather than a single rule for the whole task.
Disclosure
A student's account of whether, where and how AI was used. It is a statement, not a proof.
Verification
The action of checking whether an AI output is accurate, exists, or applies to the context.
Critical judgement
Deciding what to accept, change or reject, with reasons grounded in disciplinary knowledge.
Revision
Substantive change to a draft that improves its argument, accuracy or fit to purpose.
Student ownership
The extent to which a student can explain, defend and account for the reasoning in the submitted work.
Process evidence
Material showing how work developed — drafts, notes, verification records — as distinct from the final product.
Intended learning outcome
What a student should be able to do at the end of a module, stated so that it can be assessed.
Rubric alignment
The correspondence between what is asked for, what is submitted, and what the criteria reward.
Procedural compliance
Whether the published requirements were followed — separate from the quality of the work.
Academic-integrity process
The formal institutional route for a possible breach. It is not a decision a marker makes alone.
Proportionate evidence
The minimum material needed to support a fair judgement, given the weighting and the outcomes.

Each card is designed for an eight-minute cycle:

  1. 1 minute — read. Read the card aloud. Do not discuss yet.
  2. 1 minute — vote. Everyone votes privately before any comment.
  3. 2 minutes — separate known from unknown. Ask only: what do we know, and what are we assuming?
  4. 2 minutes — agree a proportionate response. Not the "right" answer; the smallest sufficient one.
  5. 1 minute — name one assignment-design improvement.
  6. 1 minute — report. One sentence per group.

The voting step matters. Without it, the first confident speaker sets the frame. Turn on Facilitator mode in the scenario library to see timing, discussion risks and the eight-question decision framework inside each card.

  • AI detection is not treated anywhere in this toolkit as proof of anything.
  • Process evidence does not prove authorship.
  • Disclosure does not automatically demonstrate achievement or integrity.
  • Extensive AI use does not automatically deserve a low grade; limited use does not automatically deserve a high one.
  • Not every AI-related concern is misconduct. Some are design problems.
  • No institutional policy is asserted, paraphrased or invented here. Check your own programme requirements.

This is a capstone prototype: a proposed educational design, not an official HKU policy, and not a validated instrument. It has not been evaluated with students. Its value at this stage is as a concrete, arguable proposal that a programme team could pilot and critique.
